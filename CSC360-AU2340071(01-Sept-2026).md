## 1. Vector

## 2. Column Vector

- I learned that a **column vector** represents its elements vertically in a single column.
- For example, a two-dimensional vector can be represented as:

  \[
  \begin{bmatrix}
  x \\
  y
  \end{bmatrix}
  \]

- The values `x` and `y` can represent the coordinates of a point in a two-dimensional space.
- Column vectors are useful when performing matrix operations and representing coordinates in computer graphics.

---

## 3. Row Vector

- I also learned about the **row vector**, where the elements are arranged horizontally in a single row.
- A two-dimensional row vector can be represented as:

  \[
  [x \quad y]
  \]

- The main difference between a row vector and a column vector is their arrangement.
- Understanding this distinction is important because the arrangement affects how vectors can be multiplied with matrices.
- This helped me understand the importance of maintaining the correct dimensions while performing mathematical operations.

## 4. Linear Equations and Solutions

- I learned about **linear equations** and how they can be represented using variables and coefficients.
- A system of linear equations can have one or more equations involving multiple variables.
- The solution to the system is the set of values of the variables that satisfies all the equations simultaneously.
- I understood that linear equations can also be represented in matrix form, which makes it easier to solve and analyze larger systems.
- This provided a connection between algebraic equations, matrices, and vectors.

## 5. Independent Vectors

- I learned about the importance of **independent vectors** while solving systems of linear equations.
- Vectors are linearly independent when none of the vectors can be represented as a combination of the other vectors.
- Independent vectors provide different directions or information and therefore contribute unique information to a system.
- If the required vectors are not independent, some of the information becomes redundant.
- This concept helped me understand why independence is important when determining solutions and the structure of a matrix.

## 6. Rank of a Matrix

- I learned that the **rank of a matrix** represents the number of linearly independent rows or columns in the matrix.
- The rank helps us understand how much independent information is present in a matrix.
- The number of independent rows is equal to the number of independent columns.
- Rank is useful when analyzing systems of linear equations and determining whether the equations contain independent information.
- I understood that the concept of rank is closely connected to **linear independence and solutions of equations**.

## 7. Stack vs Queue

- I learned the difference between two important data structures: **Stack and Queue**.
- A **Stack** follows the **LIFO (Last In, First Out)** principle, meaning the element added last is removed first.
- A simple example of a stack is a pile of plates, where the last plate placed on top is the first one to be removed.
- A **Queue** follows the **FIFO (First In, First Out)** principle, meaning the element added first is removed first.
- A real-life example of a queue is a line of people waiting for their turn, where the person who arrives first is served first.
- Understanding these structures helped me see how different methods of organizing and accessing data are useful for different problems.

## 10. Checking Whether a Point Is Within a Circle

- I learned how to determine whether a particular point lies **inside, on, or outside a circle**.
- A circle can be defined using its centre and radius.
- If the centre of the circle is represented by `(Cx, Cy)` and a point is represented by `(x, y)`, the distance between these two points can be calculated.
- The calculated distance can then be compared with the radius of the circle.
- If the distance is less than or equal to the radius, the point lies inside or on the circle.
- If the distance is greater than the radius, the point lies outside the circle.

## 11. Using the Distance Formula

- I learned that the **distance formula** can be used to calculate the distance between two points in a two-dimensional coordinate system.
- For two points `(x₁, y₁)` and `(x₂, y₂)`, the distance is calculated using:

  \[
  d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}
  \]

- The formula is useful for solving geometric problems involving points, circles, and distances.
- In computer graphics, such calculations can be used for determining relationships between objects and coordinates.
- I understood that a simple mathematical formula can be directly applied to practical graphical problems.

---

## 12. Proof of the Distance Formula Using Pythagoras

- I learned that the distance formula is derived from the **Pythagorean theorem**.
- Consider two points `(x₁, y₁)` and `(x₂, y₂)` on a coordinate plane.
- By drawing horizontal and vertical lines between the two points, a right-angled triangle can be formed.
- The horizontal side of the triangle represents the difference between the x-coordinates:

  \[
  x_2-x_1
  \]

- The vertical side represents the difference between the y-coordinates:

  \[
  y_2-y_1
  \]

- According to the Pythagorean theorem:

  \[
  a^2+b^2=c^2
  \]

- Substituting the horizontal and vertical distances gives:

  \[
  (x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2=d^2
  \]

- Taking the square root on both sides gives the distance formula:

  \[
  d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}
  \]

- This helped me understand not only how to use the distance formula but also **where the formula comes from and why it works**.
